## In one line

Use "we" for the context and "I" for the decisions, because the only thing the interviewer can evaluate is what you personally did.

## What it is

Engineers who work on good teams default to "we". It's culturally correct and interview-fatal, because the write-up has to answer "what did this candidate do" and a story made of "we decided, we shipped, we found" doesn't answer it. The interviewer either has to interrogate you for the answer — burning your time — or guess low.

The fix is not to claim other people's work. It's a division of labour inside the sentence: **"we" carries the setting, "I" carries the actions.** "The team was three engineers on a two-month deadline. I owned the sync layer. I proposed we drop offline support for v1 because the conflict resolution was where all the risk was, and I wrote the doc that got that agreed."

Overcorrecting is a real failure too, and it shows up as a story where the candidate appears to have done everything, made every call, and had no dependencies. That reads as either exaggeration or as someone who steamrollers a team — and it's easily exposed by "what did the other two engineers do?"

The strongest version does both at once: precise about your own contribution, generous and specific about others'. "The migration plan was mine; the reason it worked was that our SRE insisted on a dry run against a prod snapshot, which caught a constraint violation I'd missed." That single sentence signals ownership, humility, and collaboration, and it's more believable than either extreme.

A pronoun audit is worth doing once, out loud, on your two or three main stories. Most people are surprised by how much of their own work disappears.

## Why it matters

This is the single most common preventable weakness in strong candidates' answers, and it maps directly to the levelling decision — a story with no identifiable "I" gets recorded as ambiguous scope, which resolves downwards. It's also cheap to fix: same stories, different pronouns.

## Key points

- The write-up must answer "what did *they* do"; "we" stories force the interviewer to guess, and they guess low.
- Set the scene in "we", state decisions and actions in "I".
- Name your specific surface — "I owned the sync layer" — rather than the project as a whole.
- Total "I" invites "so what did everyone else do?", which is a hard question to recover from.
- Credit others concretely, with what they contributed, not with a generic nod to the team.
- Combining precise self-attribution with specific credit is the most credible register.
- Audit your main stories for pronouns once; the drift is usually invisible from the inside.
