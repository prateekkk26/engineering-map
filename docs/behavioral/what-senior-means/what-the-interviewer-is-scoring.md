## In one line

The interviewer is filling in a rubric of four or five signals from your stories, and anything in your answer that doesn't feed one of them is decoration.

## What it is

Behavioural interviewing looks unstructured and usually isn't. Most companies of any maturity hand the interviewer a short list of attributes to rate with evidence, and the write-up afterwards is organised by those attributes rather than by your narrative. At the target companies the list is some version of:

**Scope and ownership** — how big was the thing that was genuinely yours, and did you stay with it past the merge. **Judgement** — did you make a real trade-off and can you name what it cost. **Collaboration and conflict** — what happens when someone disagrees with you, especially someone more senior. **Drive through ambiguity** — do you move when nobody hands you a spec. **Communication** — could a non-expert follow your explanation of your own work. Frequently a sixth: **learning**, meaning whether the story ends with something you changed about yourself.

Two consequences follow.

The first is that **the interviewer needs evidence, not conclusions.** "I'm very collaborative" rates nothing. "The staff engineer wanted to keep the sync API; I built both versions behind a flag and we compared the p95" rates collaboration and judgement in one sentence. Write-ups quote candidates; give them something quotable.

The second is that **one story can cover several signals**, which is why the story bank is small. A well-chosen story about a migration can carry scope, judgement, conflict, and communication at once. That's also why interviewers dig: the follow-ups are them hunting for the specific signal the first pass didn't cover.

Silence after your answer is usually note-taking, not disapproval. Let it happen instead of adding a fourth example.

## Why it matters

Knowing the rubric changes what you keep and what you cut under time pressure. Most candidates over-invest in technical detail — which is scored in other rounds — and under-invest in the decision, the disagreement, and the outcome, which are the only things this round can measure. It also explains why great engineers get rejected: not because the work wasn't good, but because the account of it contained no evidence for the rubric.

## Key points

- Assume a written rubric: scope, judgement, collaboration, ambiguity, communication, and often learning.
- Interviewers record evidence and quotes, so state the concrete decision rather than the self-assessment.
- Technical depth is scored elsewhere; here it's context, and too much of it crowds out the signal.
- Design stories to hit multiple signals; that's why eight good stories cover a whole loop.
- Follow-up questions are signal-hunting, not scepticism — answer the specific thing asked rather than restarting the story.
- End on what changed as a result, for the system and for you; the learning signal is cheap to earn and often skipped.
- Ambiguity has its own line item: say what you did when nobody told you what to do.
