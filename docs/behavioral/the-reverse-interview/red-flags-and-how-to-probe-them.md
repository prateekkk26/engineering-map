## In one line

Probe with questions that require a specific answer, and treat a vague response to a specific question as the finding.

## What it is

Interviews are optimised to sell you the job, so warning signs are rarely stated. They surface as evasion, inconsistency between interviewers, or a detail nobody wants to elaborate on.

**The ones worth probing:**

**Attrition.** "How long has the average engineer been here?" and "why did the last person in this role leave?" A hesitation, or three different stories from three interviewers, matters more than the content of any single answer.

**Process theatre versus chaos.** Ask how a change gets from idea to production. Either extreme is a signal: a fifteen-step process at a twenty-person company, or an answer that reveals nobody knows who decides what.

**On-call and incidents.** "How often does someone get paged out of hours?" and "what happened after the last incident?" If there's no postmortem practice, incidents repeat and you'll live inside them.

**Scope of the role.** If the job description says senior frontend and the conversation drifts to owning infrastructure, data, and design, ask what the first six months actually look like. Small companies genuinely need breadth — that's fine — but there's a difference between breadth and three unfilled roles.

**Working hours across time zones.** For a US/EU remote role, ask what meetings are fixed and at what time. "We're flexible" without specifics tends to mean 6pm calls.

**How the last hard decision went.** A team that can't name one either doesn't make them or doesn't discuss them.

**Talking about the AI hype.** Founders who can't describe what their product doesn't do are selling, and the engineering reality behind that is usually a demo held together with prompt tweaks.

Then weigh them. A single red flag is normal — everywhere has something. Two founders contradicting each other on strategy is a different order of concern. And notice how the process treats you: ghosting between rounds, last-minute rescheduling, and a take-home that's really three days of unpaid work are all previews of how the company operates internally.

## Why it matters

At a small company, culture is not a background variable — it's your daily experience and it comes directly from four or five people you'll have met. A year is a long time to spend somewhere you could have diagnosed in one careful conversation.

## Key points

- Warning signs appear as vagueness in response to specificity, so ask specific questions.
- Ask why the last person in the role left, and compare answers across interviewers.
- Ask how a change reaches production; both heavy process and total absence of it are signals.
- Ask about paging frequency and whether postmortems happen at all.
- Check that the role's scope matches the title, and ask what the first six months actually contain.
- Pin down fixed meeting times for cross-timezone roles before believing "flexible".
- One red flag is normal; contradictions between interviewers are not.
- The hiring process itself — ghosting, rescheduling, oversized take-homes — is a live sample of the company.
